# Build the practice partner as a cascade

The best way to give Conclave a spoken practice partner is a **local cascade built in Rust**, not an end-to-end speech model. The cascade has four parts: the whisper.cpp you already ship, then an 8–14B LLM through the existing `llama-helper` sidecar (or a cloud LLM on your own key), then Kokoro TTS, with Silero VAD and Smart Turn deciding when you have finished speaking. It fits in about 12–13 GB of VRAM, costs nothing per minute, keeps an exact text record of both sides for the weak-spots report, and lets you control turn-taking. That last point fixes the main complaint about ChatGPT voice mode: it cuts learners off. NVIDIA NemotronLabs VoiceChat 11B does fit by size, but **no Q4_K_M build exists**. The only consumer path is an unofficial Q4_0/Q8_0 GGUF on an 8-star llama.cpp fork that has **no system prompt, so it cannot play a professor or a doctor**. It is also English-only and labelled research-only. If you want something that feels more human than a cascade, the best paid option is **Gemini 3.8 Live**: about $0.012/min, roughly $7 for 10 hours, with a free tier and German among 97 languages. The best local full-duplex option is **NVIDIA PersonaPlex 7B via moshi.cpp**, but it loses the persona after about four minutes. Whichever engine runs the conversation, the weak-spots report needs its own post-session pass. Stock Whisper hides learner errors, so the report re-transcribes your mic audio verbatim, computes fluency metrics from timestamps, has an LLM find grammar errors that must quote your exact words, and adds pronunciation scoring from Azure only where the evidence is strong. Before writing any code, spend two evenings and under $5 testing three candidates on your own PC (Gemini Live, PersonaPlex, and an off-the-shelf local cascade), plus one test of how faithfully each transcribes deliberate mistakes.

## VoiceChat 11B fits in 16 GB but cannot hold a character

Your memory of the size is right; the details around it are not. NVIDIA released VoiceChat 11B on August 3, 2026. It is a full-duplex model (it listens and speaks at the same time), built on a Nemotron Nano v2 backbone with its own speech encoder, TTS decoder and codec. It answers a turn in about 448 ms on an H100, and its card supports system prompts ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-NemotronLabs-VoiceChat-11B)). But the official checkpoint is 44 GB, the documented runtime is vLLM on Linux, and NVIDIA's NeMo branch asks for **an 80 GB GPU** ([NVIDIA-NeMo/Speech](https://github.com/NVIDIA-NeMo/Speech/tree/nemotron-labs-voicechat)). The one GGUF on Hugging Face ships **Q8_0 (12.04 GB) and Q4_0 (6.62 GB)**. With all the speech parts included, Q4_0 comes to about 6.1 GiB. Its VRAM tiers are targets, not measurements, and the page makes no claim about quality ([hoidhxd GGUF](https://huggingface.co/hoidhxd/NVIDIA-NemotronLabs-VoiceChat-11B-GGUF)).

Stock llama.cpp cannot load that file. The fork that can, llama-voicechat.cpp, ships Windows CUDA 12.8 binaries built for your card's sm_89. That makes it the only route that needs no WSL2. Its input and output are **WAV files in and WAV files out**, with no live microphone documented. It has **no chat template or system prompt**. And it needs `VC_NO_BARGE=1` and `VC_FORCE_BOS=1`, or the model barges in about a second into a clip and later turns get worse ([llama-voicechat.cpp](https://github.com/sansamour/llama-voicechat.cpp)). Even on the official stack, coverage lists repeated or truncated speech, clarification loops and unreliable instruction following, and notes that the model is "not explicitly trained for broad reasoning" ([Yowox](https://yowox.com/posts/nvidia-nemotron-voicechat-tool-calling/)). One unverified report adds a two-minute context ceiling ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/08/09/nvidia-releases-nemotronlabs-voicechat-11b-an-open-full-duplex-speech-to-speech-model-with-450-ms-turn-taking-and-live-tool-calling/)). Nobody has published a run on a 16 GB or 24 GB consumer card. So VoiceChat 11B is worth one time-boxed hour of curiosity, not a build.

## Local full-duplex models sound alive but forget the scene

The local end-to-end models that do fit on 16 GB trade intelligence and endurance for natural turn-taking. **PersonaPlex-7B** (January 2026, built on Kyutai's Moshi) is the most practical. A text prompt sets the role, background and scenario, and a WAV clip sets the voice. On Full-Duplex-Bench it beats VoiceChat on smooth turn-taking (0.908 vs 0.82), and its licence allows commercial use ([HF personaplex-7b-v1](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/personaplex-7b-v1)). moshi.cpp runs it natively on Windows 10 and 11 with CUDA, in q8_0 or q4_k, and Moshi at q4_k benchmarks under 8 GB ([moshi.cpp](https://github.com/Codes4Fun/moshi.cpp)). One hands-on test on an RTX 3090 (bf16, about 19 GB) called it "astonishing" at first. But its context is about 240 seconds, after which **the persona forgot its own name and started looping** ([MakeUseOf](https://www.makeuseof.com/nvidia-personaplex-local-speech-model-8gb-vram/)). Ten-minute doctor's appointments would have to be split into short scenes that re-inject the prompt. It also produces no transcript of your side, so Conclave's Whisper would still have to run alongside it.

**MiniCPM-o 4.5** (9B, Apache-2.0) is the smart option. It is built on Qwen3-8B, supports full-duplex streaming and voice-cloned role-play, and needs about 11 GB at int4 or 12 GB through llama.cpp-omni. However, it speaks only English and Chinese, and its docs cover macOS and Ubuntu, not Windows ([HF MiniCPM-o-4_5](https://huggingface.co/openbmb/MiniCPM-o-4_5)). Qwen3-Omni is the only open model found that speaks German, and it needs about 79 GB with speech output on ([QwenLM/Qwen3-Omni](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-Omni)). **No German-speaking end-to-end model fits in 16 GB.**

| Model | Fits 16 GB with Whisper | Windows path | Persona control | Speech languages | Licence | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PersonaPlex 7B (moshi.cpp q4_k/q8_0) | Likely | Native binaries | Text prompt + voice WAV | English | NVIDIA Open Model, commercial OK | Drifts and loops after ~4 min; shallow |
| VoiceChat 11B (GGUF Q4_0) | Likely (unmeasured) | Native fork, WAV turns only | None in the fork | English | OpenMDW, "research only" | No persona, known loops |
| MiniCPM-o 4.5 (int4/GGUF) | Borderline (11–12 GB) | Unverified | Text + reference audio | English, Chinese | Apache-2.0 | Windows untested, tight VRAM |
| Qwen3-Omni 30B-A3B | No (~79 GB) | — | Yes | 10, incl. German | Apache-2.0 | Too big |

## A Rust cascade costs nothing per minute and keeps every word

A cascade is a pipeline of three separate models: speech-to-text, then an LLM, then text-to-speech. **Speech recognition is no longer the bottleneck.** Whisper large-v3-turbo, Parakeet and Canary all sit around 6–7% word error rate ([benchmarklist Open ASR](https://benchmarklist.com/benchmarks/open_asr_leaderboard/)). The real lever is deciding when you have finished speaking. **Pipecat Smart Turn v3.2** is an 8 MB BSD-2 model that covers 23 languages, including English and German. It runs in about 10–65 ms after Silero VAD (voice activity detection) detects silence, and it reruns if you start talking again ([pipecat-ai/smart-turn](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/smart-turn)). For a learner, that matters more than raw speed. Learners pause mid-clause by nature, and the most-cited complaint about ChatGPT voice is that it cuts in "while they are still forming a thought" ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/chatgpt-live-voice-still-interupts-too-much-what-happened-to-hold-to-talk/1386172)). A cascade you build yourself can add hold-to-talk or a long silence tolerance. A vendor's turn detector cannot be tuned that way.

For the LLM, budget about 8–11 GB. That means a 12–14B model at Q4: an RTX 4070 Ti SUPER is estimated at about 45 tok/s with Qwen3 14B Q4_K_M ([ModelFit](https://modelfit.io/can-i-run/qwen3-14b-q4-on-rtx-4070-ti-super/)). The 26–27B models that 16 GB guides recommend take the whole card alone ([runaihome](https://www.runaihome.com/blog/best-local-llm-16gb-vram-2026/)). Turn off "thinking" modes, because a reasoning preamble adds seconds before the first spoken word. Conclave already calls cloud LLMs with your keys, so pointing the cascade at Groq or Claude frees about 8 GB and often gets the first token out faster. The cost is a few cents of privacy, which ranks last among your priorities. For TTS, **Kokoro 82M** (Apache-2.0) runs 35–100x faster than real time on a 4060 Ti-class card. On the Artificial Analysis open-weights arena it scores 1061 Elo, statistically tied with NVIDIA's Magpie and ahead of Chatterbox and XTTS v2 ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/text-to-speech/leaderboard/provider-voice/open-weights)). It has 54 preset voices for distinct characters, but **no German** ([Kokoro VOICES.md](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M/blob/main/VOICES.md)). For German later, **Chatterbox Multilingual V3** (MIT, 500M, 23 languages including German) is the drop-in replacement, though every output carries a watermark ([resemble-ai/chatterbox](https://github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox)).

| Component | Choice | Approx. VRAM |
|---|---|---|
| Speech-to-text | whisper.cpp large-v3-turbo, language pinned to `en` | ~2 GB |
| End of turn | Silero VAD + Smart Turn v3.2 (ONNX via `ort`) | <0.1 GB |
| LLM | Gemma 4 12B or Qwen3 14B Q4_K_M, 8K context, via `llama-helper` (or a cloud key) | ~9–10 GB (0 if cloud) |
| TTS | Kokoro 82M ONNX (English); Chatterbox Multilingual V3 for German | ~0.5–1 GB; Chatterbox a few GB (unverified) |
| **Total** | | **~12–13 GB**, leaving room for the Windows desktop |

These sizes come from component estimates. Nobody has measured this combination, so check it with `nvidia-smi`. Kyutai's Unmute reference stack is the nearest real-world anchor: it uses about 14 GB, but with only a 1B LLM. Run on one shared GPU, its TTS alone slowed to about 750 ms ([kyutai-labs/unmute](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/unmute)). Adding up the streamed stages gives a realistic **voice-to-voice latency of about 0.7–1.3 s**. That figure is an estimate, not a measurement. Paid speech-to-speech APIs measure **0.8–1.4 s to first audio** on Artificial Analysis ([Artificial Analysis S2S](https://artificialanalysis.ai/speech-to-speech)), so a well-built local cascade does not lose on speed. It loses on prosody, overlap and back-channelling, which is why a cascade sounds like "a fast, polite turn-taker" rather than a person ([Coval](https://www.coval.ai/blog/speech-to-speech-vs-cascaded-voice-ai-which-architecture-should-you-deploy/)).

On Windows, none of the Python frameworks (Pipecat, LiveKit, RealtimeVoiceChat) embed inside Tauri, and Unmute is Linux or WSL2 only. Building the cascade natively in Rust reuses whisper.cpp, the existing llama sidecar, cpal capture and Conclave's VAD, and avoids the overhead of passing the GPU through to WSL2. WSL2 is still useful for the bake-off below, just not for the product.

## Paid realtime APIs cost $7–66 per ten hours, and Gemini leads on price

If you want a partner that feels human, paying is cheap at personal scale. Google's **Gemini 3.8 Live** bills $0.005/min for your speech and $0.018/min for its speech, and the Live models have a free tier where "input and output are free of charge" ([Gemini API pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)). Free tiers may use your data to improve products, so check the terms before relying on it. Gemini 3.8 Live scores 76.0 on the Artificial Analysis speech-to-speech index (82.6 in Extended Thinking mode). That puts it near OpenAI's GPT-Live-1 (80–83, $0.05/min plus a backend model) and below xAI's Grok Voice 3.0 (86.9) ([Artificial Analysis S2S](https://artificialanalysis.ai/speech-to-speech); [OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). Gemini offers 30 voices and 97 languages ([Micdrop](https://micdrop.dev/blog/gpt-live-vs-gpt-realtime-vs-gemini-live)). In a casual persona role-play, one consumer test found it more natural and energetic than ChatGPT ([Tom's Guide](https://tomsguide.com/ai/i-challenged-gemini-live-vs-chatgpt-in-5-voice-challenges-there-was-one-clear-winner)). GPT-Live-1 is the API form of the ChatGPT voice experience you already dislike, so it is the riskiest premium choice.

The naive figures assume a 50/50 talk split and come from [Micdrop](https://micdrop.dev/blog/gpt-live-vs-gpt-realtime-vs-gemini-live) and [Forasoft](https://www.forasoft.com/article/openai-realtime-api-pricing). Gemini caps each connection at about 10–15 minutes, so a 30-minute session needs context-window compression and session resumption in code ([Gemini session management](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-api/session-management)). The integration itself is easy. Rust mints an ephemeral token, and the WebView2 front end connects over WebSocket or WebRTC, which also gives you the browser's echo cancellation. The endpoint has to be added to `docs/network-calls.md` and the no-phone-home allow-list.

## The weak-spots report needs its own verbatim pass

Whatever engine runs the conversation, **its transcript of your speech is not good enough to grade you**. On 50 hours of spontaneous learner English, Whisper had a word error rate of 18–19%. Most of the errors in whisper-small.en came from exactly the features a report needs: fillers, self-repairs and ungrammatical forms that the model quietly "fixed". Large-v3 did *worse* than small.en, because it heard accents as other languages ([LearnerVoice](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.04280)). Vendor transcripts behave the same way. OpenAI's realtime transcription uses earlier turns as context, and forum advice is to treat it as "guidance" ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/transcription-errors-in-realtime-api/1367938)). Gemini documents a VERBATIM mode that keeps fillers and false starts, but ties it to a separate transcription model ([Gemini live transcription](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-api/live-transcribe)). **No vendor documents preserving grammar errors.**

The report should therefore come from a post-session pass over your own mic track, recorded locally (Conclave already captures it).

**Transcription.** Re-transcribe your turns with the language forced to English, `condition_on_previous_text=False`, and a hesitation-seeded prompt ("Um, uh, er…"). Prompting surfaces disfluencies but can make Whisper hallucinate or loop ([McGuire 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06924)). The stronger option is **CrisperWhisper**, a verbatim Whisper fine-tune with accurate word timestamps. It reports 87.8–93.5 disfluency F1 in its vendor's own benchmark and is licensed CC-BY-NC, which is fine for personal use ([CrisperWhisper](https://github.com/nyrahealth/CrisperWhisper)). No whisper.cpp port of it was confirmed, so it would run as a Python step after the session.

**Fluency.** From the word timestamps, compute the standard measures of complexity, accuracy and fluency (CAF): speech rate, articulation rate, mean length of run, filled pauses and repairs per 100 words, and pauses split into mid-clause and end-of-clause. Mid-clause pausing is the clearest sign of L2 formulation difficulty ([UCL AP2018](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10066019/1/AP2018.pdf)). Native speakers vary widely on these measures, so compare yourself with your own earlier sessions, not with native norms ([IRAL 2022](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2022-0125/html)).

**Grammar.** Have a large cloud LLM, at temperature 0, use a fixed error taxonomy with few-shot examples. Make every finding quote an exact span, then check in code that the span exists in the transcript. This is the pattern Comuniqa uses ([arXiv 2401.15595](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.15595)). Group recurring errors ("articles missed 7×") rather than listing every slip. Show the CEFR level only as a rough trend: prompt-only CEFR rating is inconsistent and gets worse at intermediate and advanced grammar ([Univ. Barcelona](https://diposit.ub.edu/bitstreams/a939d13e-e9d6-42f0-9cc9-81601b991824/download)).

**Pronunciation.** Feed the confirmed verbatim text into **Azure Pronunciation Assessment** as scripted reference text, one turn under 30 s at a time. Microsoft recommends this over its unscripted mode. The richer outputs (prosody, phoneme names, syllables) are **en-US only** ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/how-to-pronunciation-assessment)). Reported cost is about $0.30/hr on top of $1/hr STT; this is a third-party figure ([Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-in/answers/questions/5608069/pricing-and-usage-of-pronunciation-assessment-feat)). Local scorers reach a phone-level correlation of only about 0.7 with human raters ([arXiv 2302.10444](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.10444)), so present them as "possible problem words", never as grades.

**Feedback timing.** Research shows no clear advantage for immediate over delayed correction, and learners say they are uncomfortable being corrected mid-sentence ([ERIC EJ1167213](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1167213.pdf)). Let the character stay in role and save corrections for the report. Reviews of Praktika specifically fault it for having no end-of-lesson error list ([copycatcafe](https://copycatcafe.com/blog/praktika-review)).

## Recommendation and a two-evening bake-off

Build the conversation engine behind a small interface. Make the **local Rust cascade the default** (whisper.cpp, then an LLM via `llama-helper` or a cloud key, then Kokoro, with Silero and Smart Turn plus hold-to-talk). Keep **Gemini 3.8 Live as an optional "natural" engine** if the cascade feels too stiff. The report pipeline runs the same way whichever engine is chosen. This order follows your priorities. The cascade costs nothing per minute. It gives the most control over turn-taking, character level and voices, and the clearest route to German by swapping Kokoro for Chatterbox. And it keeps your speech on your machine. Leave PersonaPlex and VoiceChat 11B as experiments until a German-capable full-duplex model that fits 16 GB appears.

Test before building; nothing below needs new Conclave code, and the whole bake-off costs about $1. Run the same scripted scene in every candidate: a mechanic diagnosing a grinding noise, about 8 minutes, with deliberate long pauses mid-sentence. For each, record peak `nvidia-smi` VRAM, whether the character survives past minute four, and 1–5 scores for naturalness and "did it cut me off".

| When | Candidate | How to run it |
|---|---|---|
| Evening 1 | Gemini 3.8 Live | Google AI Studio, free tier, persona system prompt |
| Evening 1 | gpt-realtime-2.1-mini | OpenAI playground, a few cents; checks whether your ChatGPT dislike carries over |
| Evening 1 | PersonaPlex 7B | moshi.cpp Windows binary, q8_0 then q4_k, `-p` persona, `-c 1000` if it slows |
| Evening 2 | Local cascade | KoljaB's RealtimeVoiceChat (Ollama + Qwen3 14B Q4 + Kokoro, manual Windows install), or Kyutai Unmute in WSL2 with `KYUTAI_LLM_URL` pointed at Ollama; record both channels and read the mouth-to-ear gap in Audacity to see whether 0.7–1.3 s holds on your card |
| Evening 2, optional | VoiceChat 11B | One hour, no more: llama-voicechat.cpp CUDA zip, Q4_0, WAV turns, `VC_NO_BARGE=1 VC_FORCE_BOS=1` |
| Evening 2 | Faithfulness test | Ten recorded sentences with planted errors ("he go to doctor yesterday", "I am here since three years") plus fillers and restarts, transcribed by whisper.cpp turbo with and without the hesitation prompt, and by Gemini's input transcription |

The faithfulness test matters most. Count how many planted errors each transcript keeps; that count decides how much of the verbatim report pipeline you need to build.

## Conclusion

For a speaking-practice partner, an end-to-end model's naturalness is not the decisive feature. What a learner needs is a partner that waits through mid-clause pauses, stays in character for ten minutes, and leaves an honest record of what was actually said. The local cascade wins on all three, at no cost per minute. The local full-duplex models currently fail on persona length or persona control, and the paid APIs' turn detection is the very behaviour you disliked in ChatGPT. The deeper finding is that the weak-spots report, not the conversation, is the hard engineering problem. Every transcription layer, local or cloud, is trained to make speech look fluent. So the report needs a deliberately verbatim second pass whatever engine you pick, and the planted-error test is the cheapest way to learn how much of that pass you must build.

Uncertainties to keep in mind: no source measured any of these stacks on an RTX 4070 Ti SUPER; several 2026 model names (Gemma 4, Qwen3.x) come only from SEO-style guides; and the Azure and xAI prices are third-party figures.
